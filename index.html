Rethinking the Berkeley EECS Website

Team Group 15
Members Brian Tamuel, Paydreanne Hinton, James Pulliam, Lakshna Dhanaraj
Target Site eecs.berkeley.edu
Scope 9 Assignments → Full Hi-Fi Prototype

The UC Berkeley EECS website is a real academic department homepage — but it had some pretty serious usability problems that were making life harder for students and visitors than it needed to be. Here's the full story of how we found those issues, designed solutions, tested them, and shipped a working prototype.

01

The Problem

When we first started evaluating candidate websites for this project, we were looking for sites that had real, diagnosable usability problems — not just ones that looked a little dated. Berkeley EECS jumped out immediately. It's used by students, prospective applicants, researchers, and faculty on a daily basis, which made the usability issues we found that much more impactful.

Our heuristic evaluation (Assignment 2) surfaced five distinct violations of Nielsen's usability heuristics. The core experience problem: users couldn't reliably navigate the site or understand what was happening after they interacted with it. Navigation was inconsistent, menus were cluttered with jargon, and clicking a link would just throw you at a blank white screen — no loading indicator, nothing.

Visibility of System Status User Control & Freedom Match with Real World Aesthetic & Minimalist Design Consistency & Standards
Heuristic #1

No feedback on navigation

Button clicks produced a blank white screen with zero loading indicator. Users had no way to know their click was registered or where they were going.

Heuristic #2

Acronym overload

Navigation used terms like BEARS, ASE, and NSI with no explanation. New users were just left guessing — not a great welcome mat for incoming students.

Heuristic #3

Dropdown menus with no escape

Clicking a nav item opened a dropdown with no close button. Scrolling didn't close it. Users had to discover that clicking elsewhere was the only way out.

Heuristic #4

Inconsistent menu behavior

Some menus opened dropdowns, some navigated directly, some stayed open, some closed. You'd basically have to relearn how navigation worked every time.

Heuristic #8

Cluttered, non-minimalist layout

The footer had redundant links (e.g. "For Students," "For Grads," and "For Undergrads" all listed separately). The homepage crammed too much into too little space.

02

The Design Process

We approached this in deliberate stages — critique first, then concept, then progressively higher-fidelity designs. The five goals we set going into redesign (Assignment 3) drove almost every decision that came after.

  • Lower cognitive overload by adding whitespace and reducing immediately visible options in the "What's Happening" and "Upcoming Events" sections.
  • Improve readability by replacing unexplained acronyms (BEARS, ASE, NSI) with their full program names.
  • Make dropdown menus easier by showing fewer links and adding a clear close mechanism.
  • Add a loading screen that tells users where they're being taken and confirms their click registered.
  • Slim down the footer by combining redundant links — no more "For Students / For Grads / For Undergrads" listed three separate times.

Low-Fidelity Sketches

We started with paper sketches (Assignment 3) to work through layout ideas without getting bogged down in tools. The big structural ideas that came out of this phase were: a full-width banner image replacing the awkward split layout, a horizontal card format for "Latest News" (replacing the cramped square thumbnails), and collapsible footer sections to kill the link clutter.

Wireframes

The Figma wireframes (Assignment 5) translated the sketches into something testable. Key additions at this stage included a search results page (which didn't exist on the original site at all), a proper loading screen, and a dark mode variant. The wireframes were built out for both desktop and mobile, with the mobile version using a single-column layout and a condensed footer.

One intentional decision here: renaming "What's Happening" to "Latest News." Peer testing later confirmed this was the right call — users were constantly confusing the two sections.

03

Testing & Iterations

We ran two rounds of usability testing — one on the low-fidelity prototype (Assignment 4) and one on the wireframes (Assignment 6). Both rounds involved think-aloud sessions where testers completed tasks without any guidance from us.

Round 1 — Low-Fidelity Testing (Assign. 4)

The early peer testing results were honestly a bit humbling. None of our testers figured out the site was an academic department homepage — everyone assumed it was some kind of events or news website. That was a pretty clear signal that the design needed a stronger identity signal up front.

"Looks like some kind of event website, maybe like a scheduling thing... It could be news or it could be events?"

— Participant, Round 1 think-aloud

Other key findings from Round 1:

  • Card interactivity was unclear — users couldn't tell what was clickable in the Latest News section.
  • Horizontal scrolling for events on mobile was not intuitive at all; users expected vertical scrolling.
  • Square thumbnails read as static, not interactive. Rectangular cards tested significantly better.

Round 2 — Wireframe Testing (Assign. 6)

Four testers (Dylan, Tirsha, Ryin, Hanson) ran through three tasks on the Figma wireframes: searching for a news article, RSVPing for an event, and toggling dark mode.

Dylan
Completed all tasks with 0 misclicks. The gray border on the Latest News section gave clear visual separation. Noted dark mode was in different spots on desktop vs. mobile — slightly confusing.
Tirsha
Missed the RSVP button on mobile; wanted it on the right side. Suggested the moon icon become an explicit toggle, not just an icon. Recommended a font size bump for accessibility.
Ryin
Completed tasks quickly. The one issue: the search bar didn't navigate to the search results wireframe — a prototype wiring issue, not a design flaw. Wanted inverted colors on the sun/moon toggle state.
Hanson
Tried clicking the event card square directly on mobile instead of the RSVP button. On desktop, the "RSVP / Details" label confused him — he looked for two separate buttons expecting booking and details to be distinct.

Design Changes Made After Testing

  • Fixed
    Dark mode toggle moved into the hamburger menu on mobile. Having it in two different spots depending on device was quietly confusing users — consolidating it removed that inconsistency.
  • Removed
    RSVP button removed from mobile event cards. Testers kept tapping the thumbnail instead of the button. Made the whole card tappable — going with what users were already naturally trying to do.
  • Fixed
    Button label simplified from "RSVP / Details" to just "RSVP". Users got confused expecting two separate buttons. Singular purpose, singular label — much less friction.
  • Added
    Clearer branding in the header (department name made more prominent) to immediately communicate this is an academic department site, not a news or events platform.

05

Final Testing & Outcomes

Assignment 8 was our final round of user testing — three participants (Jason, Rico, Nat) used the high-fidelity prototype to complete the same core tasks. Overall, the results were much cleaner than earlier rounds, which was a good sign that the iterations actually worked.

Issue (original) Status Notes
No loading feedback on navigation Resolved Loading screen with animated dots + destination label well-received by all three testers. Zero confusion.
Unexplained acronyms in nav Resolved Full program names throughout. No tester flagged this as a problem in Round 3.
Dropdown menus with no close mechanism Resolved Redesigned dropdown pushes content down instead of overlapping it; arrow indicator flips on click to signal state.
Cluttered footer with redundant links Resolved Footer consolidated; "For Students / Grads / Undergrads" merged into "Resources for Students."
Only one path to RSVP for events Partial Jason (P1) still initially navigated to Academics looking for the RSVP. Prototype scope limited how many paths we could wire up. This is a known improvement area.
RSVP form missing submit button Partial Caught by Nat (P3) during hi-fi testing — the submit button was omitted from the prototype. Documented for resolution.

"The design feels clean and easy to navigate. Nothing was hard to find."

— Nat (P3), Assignment 8 testing

The dark mode toggle worked particularly well across all testers — everyone found it immediately without being told where to look. The loading screen was another success: where the original site had given users a blank white screen and no feedback, testers in Round 3 navigated confidently with no confusion about where their clicks were taking them.

The two remaining issues (RSVP discoverability and the missing submit button) are genuinely fixable — they're prototype completeness issues more than design problems. If this were a live build, both would be quick wins.

What We Actually Learned

Probably the most useful thing from this whole process: usability testing consistently surfaced problems we genuinely didn't see ourselves. We went into wireframe testing expecting the dark mode toggle to be the confusing part — it wasn't. The RSVP button on mobile was way more of a problem than we anticipated. That kind of disconnect between designer expectations and user behavior is exactly why you test, and exactly why testing on real people (not just your group members) matters.

The iterative loop — design, test, find problems, fix, repeat — also made each round of revisions feel grounded in actual evidence rather than just group opinion. When we changed the RSVP button behavior on mobile, it wasn't a guess. We'd watched four people independently try to tap the card instead of the button.
